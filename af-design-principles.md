# AF Design Principles
**Scope:** Core knowledge. Applies to all Additive Force outputs — presentations, documents, reports, digital, and any future format.

---

## 01 / One job per output unit

Every slide, page, or screen makes one claim. The primary element states it. The supporting element confirms it. If a unit is trying to do two things, it should be two units. When in doubt, ask: what is the single thing a viewer should take away? Everything else is noise.

## 02 / Evidence supports claim

Primary and supporting elements have a defined relationship. The primary states the argument. The supporting provides the evidence. A chart without a claim is data. A claim without evidence is assertion. Together they are a complete unit. This relationship is not decorative — it is the structural logic of every layout.

## 03 / Information is the content

There is no decoration. No borders for visual interest. No background shapes that carry no information. No graphic elements added to fill space or signal effort. Every mark answers the question: what does this tell the reader? If it has no answer, it does not appear. Restraint is not absence — it is discipline.

Borders are informational, not decorative. When structure is already communicated by proximity and alignment, borders are omitted. When a border appears, it communicates separation, grouping, or containment — and nothing else.

## 04 / Whitespace is structural

Empty space is not wasted space. It is what allows the eye to read hierarchy, move between elements, and rest before the next claim. Crowding does not make something more informative — it makes it less readable. When content feels sparse, the instinct to fill is almost always wrong. Generous padding inside tables, cards, and panels is always correct.

## 05 / Colour signals, it does not decorate

Colour has one job: to tell the reader something. Blue marks what matters. Charcoal recedes. White and Deep Indigo are grounds. Every use of colour should answer: what am I being told about the importance or type of this element? If everything is blue, nothing is emphasised. If colour appears without communicative purpose, remove it.

## 06 / Hierarchy before content

A well-constructed layout communicates its structure before the viewer reads a word. The eye reads size, weight, position, and colour before it reads text. This sequence should be legible in under five seconds without reading anything. If it isn't, the hierarchy has failed.

## 07 / Rhythm through variation

Sequential outputs — presentations, reports, long documents — are experienced over time. Identical units in sequence become monotonous regardless of content quality. Vary layout, weight, and theme to create visual rhythm. Let the content determine the rhythm, not a rigid rule.

## 08 / Data deserves clarity

Charts and data visualisations are not decoration — they are arguments made visible. Apply the same discipline as everything else: remove everything that doesn't carry information, use colour to direct attention not differentiate arbitrarily, label directly where possible. A well-designed chart makes the analysis self-evident. The right visual choices do the work. There is almost nothing that needs saying.

## 09 / Consistency is credibility

Inconsistency signals carelessness even when the content is good. Sizes that vary for no reason, alignment that drifts, colours applied without system — these erode trust in the thinking behind the work. Systems exist to enforce consistency without requiring constant manual judgment. Use them absolutely.

## 10 / Every decision serves the argument

Layout choices, colour choices, type choices — all exist in service of the argument being made. Choose everything for a reason. When asked why a decision was made, the answer should always trace back to what the content required.
